01 – What you are sending

Document or parcel is not a size question

It is a customs question, and it is the first thing that changes how your shipment is handled. Paper with no commercial value travels differently from anything a border can put a price on — even if the box is smaller.

Largest

Parcel

Middle

Small parcel

Document

Document

A document

Paper with no resale value — contracts, certificates, deeds, tender submissions. Usually the simplest customs treatment there is, because there is nothing to value or classify.

A parcel

Anything with commercial value, however small. It needs a description, a value and a commodity code, and it can attract duty and tax in the destination.

The trap

Putting a sample, a USB drive or a promotional item in with paperwork turns a document into a parcel. Declared as documents, it will be stopped — and the delay costs more than declaring it would have.

No dimensions or weight limits are published here. They differ by service, destination and aircraft, and a limit quoted on a web page is the one people plan around. Your quote carries the actual figures for the service you pick.

02 – Pricing

Why a light box can cost more than a heavy one

The single most common surprise on an express invoice, and it is not a mistake. Aircraft run out of space long before they run out of lift, so express services price on whichever of two numbers is larger.

Input A

Actual weight

What the scales say when the packed box goes on them.

Input B

Volumetric weight

A weight derived from the space the box occupies. Length by width by height, divided by a set figure.

Whichever is greater
You are billed on

Chargeable weight

Which is why a box of cushions costs more to fly than its scales suggest, and why trimming an oversized carton can cut a bill more than removing items would.

We have not published the divisor. It differs by service and carrier, and a number on a page that your quote contradicts is how invoice arguments start. Ask at booking — and if a box is close to the line, we will tell you whether repacking is worth the effort.

03 – What you can send

Three answers, and the middle one matters most

Most refused shipments are not people trying to send something forbidden. They are people who did not know an ordinary item needed declaring.

Documents and general goods

Paperwork, clothing, books, most consumer items, spare parts and samples. Described and valued honestly, these move without difficulty.

Anything containing a battery

Phones, laptops, tools, toys, power banks. Regulated as dangerous goods, with rules that differ depending on whether the battery is loose, in the device or packed beside it.

Liquids, aerosols and cosmetics

Perfume, nail varnish, some cleaning products. Frequently regulated by air even in small quantities, and frequently sent by people who had no idea.

Food, plants and anything biological

Subject to import controls that vary enormously by destination. Some countries refuse items others wave through without comment.

High-value items and jewellery

Not prohibited, but they change the routing, the security handling and the cover you need. Under-declaring to save duty voids the insurance you were relying on.

Cash, negotiable instruments and precious metals

Not carried on express services. There are specialist providers for this and we will point you to that conversation rather than take the booking.

Live animals, human remains and hazardous waste

Outside what an express network handles. Each requires specialist carriers, permits and handling that a parcel service is not built for.

Counterfeit goods and anything illegal at either end

Refused, and where required reported. This includes items legal where you are and not where they are going — the destination's law governs.

This is a guide, not the rule book. What is permitted depends on the destination country, the commodity and the service, and it changes. If you are unsure, describe the item before you pack it — a two-minute question beats a shipment held at an airport.

04 – Packing

Assume it will be at the bottom of the stack

Express parcels are sorted mechanically, moved on belts and loaded with hundreds of others. Nothing here is exotic — it is the difference between a claim and a delivery.

Rule 01

A new box, not a reused one

Cardboard loses most of its strength after one journey. An old box with old labels also gets scanned wrongly more often than people expect.

Rule 02

Nothing touching a wall

Cushioning on every side, including underneath. Damage almost always starts at a corner that had nothing behind it.

Rule 03

No movement when shaken

If contents shift, they will shift for the whole journey. Fill the voids rather than adding a bigger box — that costs you volumetric weight too.

Rule 04

Tape every seam

Across the middle and along both edges. Parcel tape, not household tape, and never string or wrapping paper on the outside.

Rule 05

The address inside as well as out

If a label is torn off in a sorting hub, a copy inside the box is the only thing that reunites it with you.

Rule 06

Describe the contents accurately

On the paperwork, in plain words. "Gift" and "sample" mean nothing to a customs officer and slow the shipment down rather than speeding it up.
