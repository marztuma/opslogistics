Duty changes, and what they do to your landed cost.

A tariff change is a rate shift. It is a rate on one product that has arrived. The page tracks which tariff changes affect your shipments and which ones do not. How much it costs, and which is lost or picked up depends on six things.

Supply chains built around a tariff rate can break if the rate changes. A 5% change on a high-value product can shift your sourcing decision entirely.

Six fields decide whether it affects you

Most tariff changes hit specific products on specific routes. What matters is whether your shipment matches all six criteria. If it does not match any one of them, the change does not touch your cost.

(1) Zone

What commodity zone

+

Every product gets classified into a commodity zone. The tariff change applies to that zone. If your product is in zone 8765, but the change is for zone 8766, it does not hit you.

(2) Zone

Where the goods-origin countries

+

The same product from China faces a different tariff than the same product from Mexico. If the change applies to one origin but not another, you only get hit if you source from the specified origin.

(3) Zone

Where source country is applying

+

A tariff change in the US market does not touch the EU. Know where the change applies and whether you move goods through that region.

(4) Zone

The date the note will come effect

+

Tariff changes have effective dates. Goods shipped before the date use the old rate. Goods shipped after use the new rate. The date matters for your supply chain planning.

(5) Zone

Whether some cards are exempt

+

Not every shipment from the origin pays the published rate. Free trade agreements, origin certifications, and special programs carve out exceptions.

(6) Zone

Whether a shade will switch

+

Some products shift classification under new rules. Your product might reclassify into a different zone with a different tariff rate applied.

What a duty change actually moves

Landed cost

The total cost to get the product to your door. A tariff change moves this up or down depending on the rate shift.

Who absorbs it

You or your supplier. The conversation has to happen early. A late tariff change breaks your margin plan.

Whether your price is high

If a tariff rises and your price does not, your margin shrinks. If you raise price to cover it, demand might drop.

Whether preference expires

A free trade agreement expires. A special rate ends. Your source country preference changes when the rate changes.

When you renegotiate

A tariff change is your moment to renegotiate supplier contracts. The cost moved. Your terms should too.

Whether the line will work

Some product lines become uneconomical when tariffs rise. The spreadsheet that worked at the old rate breaks at the new rate.

Go to the authority, not to a summary

The authorities (customs authority)

Custom authorities publish official tariff rates and schedules. These are the source of truth. A summary written later is second-hand.

The official gazette is open

Most countries publish their tariff schedules in an official gazette. That publication has legal weight. That is where the rate change is binding.

The trade agreement text matters

When a free trade agreement is about to expire, check the original treaty language. When the renewal is up. Who qualifies. What is carved out.

A Shing counsel can flag it

A customs lawyer knows the tariff code. They can read the change and tell you whether it touches your products. That is a conversation worth having before your margin breaks.
